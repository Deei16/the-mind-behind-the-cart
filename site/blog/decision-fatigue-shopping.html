Decision fatigue: why we make more impulse purchases when we're tired

Impulse purchases don't happen at random moments — they cluster. Late at night, after a long shift, or at the very end of a stressful errand-filled day, a purchase that would have seemed easy to skip that morning can suddenly feel worth it. This isn't a coincidence, and it isn't really about the product. It's about how much decision-making capacity is left by the time the purchase decision shows up.

What decision fatigue is

Decision fatigue describes the decline in the quality and self-control of decisions after a long session of decision-making. Each choice — what to wear, what to answer in an email, which task to prioritize — draws on the same limited mental resource. As that resource depletes over the course of a day, people tend to either avoid decisions altogether or default to the easiest option in front of them, which in a shopping context is very often "yes."

Why repeated decisions reduce resistance

Saying no to a purchase is itself a decision, and like any other decision, it takes mental effort to evaluate the trade-offs and hold a boundary. Early in the day, that evaluation happens almost automatically. After dozens of smaller decisions have already been made, the same evaluation takes more conscious effort — effort that may simply not be available anymore. The path of least resistance, in that state, is usually to stop resisting.

Why impulse purchases become more likely later in the day

This is why online shopping sessions late in the evening tend to produce different outcomes than the same browsing done fresh in the morning. The product hasn't changed. The reviews haven't changed. What's changed is the amount of mental energy left to question whether the purchase makes sense.

The product doesn't get more convincing at night. Your resistance just gets quieter.

Checkout displays and online shopping

Retailers, intentionally or not, often place their most impulse-friendly prompts at exactly the point where decision fatigue is highest: the checkout page. Add-on suggestions, "customers also bought" carousels, and one-click upsells all appear after a shopper has already made the harder decision of what to buy, at the exact moment their capacity to evaluate one more option is lowest.

Late-night shopping

Late-night browsing sits at the intersection of several of these effects at once: the day's decision-making capacity is nearly spent, boredom or scrolling has already primed the reward system discussed in our article on the dopamine loop, and there's no one around to ask "do you really need that?" before checkout. None of those conditions are inherently bad, but together they make late-night carts worth a second look the next morning.

Practical ways to reduce unnecessary purchasing decisions

  • Batch routine purchases earlier in the day, when evaluating options is easier, rather than leaving shopping for the tail end of a long day.
  • Use a waiting list instead of a cart for late-night browsing — save items to revisit tomorrow rather than deciding on them tonight.
  • Reduce the total number of small decisions elsewhere where possible, so more capacity is left for the decisions that actually matter.
  • Set a personal rule for checkout timing, such as not completing non-essential purchases after a certain hour.

Decision fatigue isn't a flaw to fix — it's a predictable pattern to plan around. Shifting shopping decisions to earlier, higher-capacity moments in the day is often enough to change the outcome.

For more background on how decision-making capacity depletes with repeated use, Psychology Today maintains an accessible overview of decision fatigue.

Before your next "add to cart" moment, pause for 60 seconds and ask yourself: is this a decision, or is it just the end of a long day talking?
