The endowment effect: why removing an item from your cart feels like a loss

Adding something to a cart and then removing it later can feel oddly uncomfortable — more uncomfortable, in fact, than simply choosing not to add it in the first place. That small discomfort has a name: the endowment effect, a well-studied bias in which people assign more value to things simply because they already possess them, even briefly.

What the endowment effect means

The endowment effect describes the tendency to value something more highly the moment we consider it "ours," compared to how we'd value the exact same item before ownership. Classic behavioral economics research demonstrated this with simple objects like mugs: people who were given a mug and then asked to sell it typically demanded a higher price than people who didn't own the mug were willing to pay for it — even though it was the identical object. Ownership itself seemed to shift its perceived value.

Why a shopping cart can trigger the same effect

A shopping cart occupies a strange psychological middle ground. Legally and financially, nothing in it belongs to you yet. Mentally, though, the act of clicking "add to cart" starts to function like a small claim of ownership. The item has been selected, set aside, and mentally slotted into your future — enough for a lightweight version of the endowment effect to kick in before a single dollar has changed hands.

Loss aversion

This connects closely to loss aversion, the broader pattern in which losing something feels worse than gaining an equivalent thing feels good. Once an item feels endowed — even just sitting in a cart — removing it registers less like "choosing not to buy" and more like "giving something up." Those two framings describe the exact same action, but they don't feel the same, which is exactly why one is so much harder to act on than the other.

Nothing in the cart is yours yet. It just doesn't feel that way anymore.

Why "remove from cart" can feel harder than "buy now"

This is part of why an abandoned cart is so much more persuasive than an empty product page. A retailer that simply shows you a product is asking you to consider a gain. A retailer with three items already sitting in your cart is — often without saying so directly — asking you to accept a loss if you walk away. That shift in framing alone can be enough to tip an undecided shopper toward completing the purchase.

How online interfaces can encourage this behavior

Several common e-commerce design choices lean on this pattern:

  • Persistent carts that save items across sessions, so a cart from last week still feels populated and "yours" when you return.
  • Cart reminder emails that frame an abandoned cart almost like an unfinished commitment rather than a page someone simply closed.
  • Low-stock warnings on cart items, which combine the endowment effect with a separate scarcity trigger to make removal feel even riskier.

None of these tactics are inherently deceptive — they're simply designed with a clear understanding of how ownership-adjacent feelings work. Recognizing the pattern is what makes it possible to evaluate a cart on its actual merits rather than its emotional pull. The same underlying psychology, from a different angle, is covered in our main article on why people buy things they don't need.

A simple test: if this item weren't already in your cart, would you go looking for it right now? If the answer is no, the pull to keep it is likely the endowment effect talking, not an actual need.

For a deeper explanation of the original research behind this bias, Investopedia's overview of the endowment effect is a clear, accessible starting point.

Next time you hesitate to remove something from your cart, ask whether you'd add it today from scratch — that answer usually tells you what you actually want.
